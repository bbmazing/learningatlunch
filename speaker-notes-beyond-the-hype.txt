Slide 4 — Part 1 — What is ChatGPT, really?
  • Time to open the box. What sits behind ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, all of them?
  • No math ahead — one idea, and everything else follows from it.

Slide 5 — Autocomplete, on an enormous scale
  • An LLM does one thing: given some text, predict the next word. Your phone keyboard does a tiny version of this.
  • When you ask a question it predicts the first word of a good answer, appends it, predicts the next, and repeats until done.
  • Everything impressive you have seen — essays, emails, code — comes from very good next-word prediction. Hold this picture; it explains the failures coming next.

Slide 6 — It read the internet — and kept the patterns
  • Where do good predictions come from? Training. The model read a huge slice of the public internet: books, articles, code, forums.
  • From that it learned patterns: grammar, facts, styles, how an apology email differs from a legal clause.
  • No database inside. It does not look answers up; it reconstructs them from patterns — like humming a song you have heard many times without owning the recording. Fluent, and sometimes fluently wrong.

Slide 7 — Brilliant and unreliable — at the same time
  • Strengths: anything shaped like language transformation. Draft an email, rewrite it politely, summarise a forty-page circular, translate Bahasa–English.
  • Failures: fresh events after training, precise arithmetic, and anything about your specific customers — your data was never in the training.
  • Hallucination: ask for a supporting regulation and it may cite one that does not exist. Well formatted, confident, wrong. It is predicting what a citation looks like. Rule: every fact is a draft to verify.

Slide 8 — Context in, quality out
  • The quality of the answer tracks the quality of what you put in.
  • Left: a one-line request gets a generic answer. Right: role, situation, points to cover, tone, length — same model, different result.
  • Mental model: brief it like a smart intern who joined this morning. Brilliant, fast, and knows nothing about your job until you tell it. That is 80% of prompting skill.

Slide 9 — A plain chatbot hits three walls
  • Put it together and a plain chatbot has three walls.
  • Wall one, time: knowledge stops at the training cutoff. Wall two, access: it cannot see your inbox, your core banking system, or that spreadsheet from this morning. Wall three, action: it writes words; it cannot send, search or file anything.
  • Keep these three in mind — part two is how engineers knock them down. That is the difference between a toy and the demos coming up.

Slide 10 — Part 2 — From chatbot to coworker
  • Part two. How a text predictor becomes something that does work.
  • Three ideas: tools, agents, grounding. Each demo uses at least one.

Slide 11 — Tool calling — give the model hands
  • Instead of asking the model to know everything, hand it tools: a web search, a calculator, a database query — and teach it to say: to answer this, I should use that tool.
  • Ask for the dollar rate today. The model knows its training data is stale, calls the rate lookup, gets the live number, writes the answer around it.
  • The model supplies judgment and language; the tool supplies facts and action. This one idea powers every serious AI product this year, including all four demos.

Slide 12 — An agent is a loop, not a chat
  • Now let it use tools more than once. Give it a goal instead of a question and let it loop: plan, act, check, repeat until done. That loop is an AI agent.
  • Real task from our world: screen this company for negative news. One search is never enough — you search, read, notice a same-name mismatch, refine, check the directors, then write it up.
  • An agent runs that same loop. You will watch one do exactly this in the Adverse News demo.

Slide 13 — Ground it in your documents
  • Last concept, and it fixes wall two: the model being blind to your data. Give it your documents at question time.
  • Upload files. When you ask, the system finds the relevant pages and instructs the model: answer from these pages, cite where you found it, say so if the answer is not there.
  • Turns a confident guesser into a fast reader of your files. Engineers call it RAG; the name matters less than the effect. Lumina, demo four, runs on this.

Slide 14 — Part 3 — Built here

Slide 15 — Bank Statement Analyzer
  • Pain every credit and ops person knows: statements from other banks arrive as PDFs and phone scans, dozens of pages, every bank a different format. Someone retypes transactions line by line. Hours per application.
  • The tool: upload, OCR reads the pages, the model structures every transaction, then summarises account behaviour.
  • Concepts at work: reading and restructuring text is the model’s home turf; OCR is a tool in the loop. The analyst still makes the credit judgment — the retyping is gone.

Slide 16 — Adverse News Search · 1:45
  • Setup: before we deal with a company or an individual we screen for adverse news. Done by hand: dozens of Google searches, per source, per entity — slow, unstandardized, easy to miss things. And the same-name problem.
  • In the video: two entities entered — Maktour, and a related party, Fuad Hasan Masyhur. Watch the queries preview: ten queries auto-generated across five source categories: SIPP district courts, Mahkamah Agung, negative-news keywords, sanctions and PEP lists, general news.
  • Then: results stream in parallel, a risk verdict computed from credible sources only — high, for both. The narrative names suspects and cites every source. The Combined tab cross-references the two entities: same KPK case. One click: PDF dossiers with evidence appendix and audit log, ready for the KYC file.
  • Landing: the agent loop from part two, live. The agent does the legwork; the officer makes the call — verification in minutes, not an hour of searching.

Slide 17 — MoM AI · 0:30
  • Setup: this one touches everyone. Every meeting ends the same way — someone still has to write the minutes. So somebody half-listens to type notes, and the minutes still arrive two days late.
  • In the video: drop in the recording — a committee meeting .wav — plus optional PDFs the model uses as context, and the meeting details. Four steps in the sidebar: upload, speakers, transcript, minutes.
  • Watch the speakers step: three speakers detected automatically, play a sample, name them, merge duplicates. Then the minutes: summary, attendees with roles, a five-item agenda with actions and PIC per item.
  • Landing: speech-to-text is the tool, the model drafts the structure. Toggle English or Bahasa Indonesia, regenerate a section, export PDF or DOCX. Thirty minutes of typing becomes five minutes of review — and the sign-off stays yours.

Slide 18 — LuminaLM · 0:50
  • Setup: a programme accumulates documents fast. The video opens with the honest picture: charters, playbooks, status packs, minutes — 157 documents, and the programme’s truth scattered across all of them.
  • In the video, project Hampton — a banking M&A integration. Sources compile into a living wiki: cross-linked pages, a knowledge graph, always current. Then grounded chat: ask for the strategic imperatives and the answer cites its sources, one chip per claim.
  • Also on screen: the programme timeline with every workstream, dependency and tollgate; and Studio — pick a template and it drafts the Word, Excel or PowerPoint pack from the wiki.
  • Landing: grounding from part two, in production. It answers from the documents and cites the page — and when the answer is not there, it says so instead of inventing one. A colleague who has read every project document and never forgets a page number.

Slide 19 — Four tools, one pattern
  • Four side by side, one pattern. Each is the same text predictor from part one, plus tools, plus your documents.
  • In each one the person keeps the judgment: the analyst approves the credit, the officer clears the name, you sign the minutes, the team makes the decisions.
  • What the AI removed is the reading, retyping, searching and formatting that sat between people and their judgment. Less magic than the headlines — and more useful.

Slide 20 — Start this week — inside the rules
  • How to start, and how to stay safe. Start where a rough draft has value: summarise a circular, draft a reply, translate a document, ask it to explain a term from a meeting.
  • Rule one: customer data and confidential material never go into public AI tools. Use what the bank provides and follow our data policy. Rule two: verify facts, numbers and citations before they travel — you saw why in part one.
  • Rule three: whatever you send still carries your name. Treat AI output like an intern’s draft: useful, fast, and reviewed before it leaves your desk.
